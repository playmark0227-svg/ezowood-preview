MATERIAL / 01

Where to look in the grain.

Begin discovering Hokkaido’s burl wood with a little time spent looking at the grain. Move beyond the number of fine patterns, between the whole and the detail, light and shadow, to find the character you enjoy.

EZOWOOD JOURNAL / 01

In this story

01 — Begin with the whole pen.02 — Look between the lines, too.03 — Imagine your own desk.

Begin with the whole pen.

In a close-up photograph, fine lines and gathered colours catch the eye. Yet stepping back to see the whole pen first can reveal a different impression. Where are the pale areas, and where do the darker colours gather? Does the whole appear calm, or full of movement? Take a moment to look at that first impression without hurrying to put it into words.

Then move closer to the area that interests you. Look at the lines that make up what caught your eye from a distance. The intricacy of a pattern and the calm of the whole are not the same thing. Changing the distance as you move between them offers another way to see the material.

Look between the lines, too.

Curves and dots within burl invite the eye to follow them. Shift your gaze a little, and look at the surfaces between one pattern and the next: where colour spreads softly, a line breaks, or light and shade become gentle. The quiet places that support the more striking ones have their own expression of wood.

You need no detailed knowledge to spend time with the grain. Finding one place you like can be enough. On one day you may be drawn to a dense pattern; on another, your eye may settle on a broad, quiet surface. There is no need to decide your preference all at once. Leave a little pleasure in looking again.

Imagine your own desk.

After looking at a pen on its own, imagine it beside your familiar diary or paper. White sheets, a deeply coloured cover, small metal objects. What lies alongside changes the impression of the wood’s colour. Choosing a tool can mean considering not only whether you enjoy the material, but whether you would like it within your own surroundings.

Do you often write by a window, or compose letters at a desk at night? Imagine the light where it will be used, and the grain begins to leave the photograph. Where will you keep the pen, and when will you reach for it? Let the thought move gradually from looking to using.
