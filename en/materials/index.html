THE WOOD

Within the grain, a different passage of time.

Take a closer look at Hokkaido’s burl wood: its colours, its lines. Beginning with birch burl, we introduce its character and the ways it might enter everyday life. Here are a few perspectives for enjoying the material.

01 / SHIRAKABA

A little closer to birch.

Shirakaba, also known in Japanese as shirakanba, is a deciduous broadleaf birch. It grows not only in Hokkaido, but also in central and northern Honshu and north-eastern Asia. Known for its pale bark, it is a tree you may find yourself looking for in a forest or on a journey once you know its name. Its yellowing leaves in autumn show another side of the same tree.

Birch wood is also used for implements, musical instruments and furniture. A tree standing in a forest takes on another form in human life. Thinking about that connection, a writing instrument becomes one of the ways this material can be used. As a brand introducing Hokkaido’s burl wood, we have chosen birch burl for our first product concept.

02 / BURL

The beauty of lines that do not run straight.

A burl is a knot-like growth that can form on a tree’s trunk, around its base or elsewhere. Its patterns cannot be followed as simply as a band of stripes. A bending line, a gathering of dots, a boundary between light and dark: once you begin looking, your eye finds its way from one place to another.

Fine detail is not the only pleasure in looking. Quiet areas between the lines and places where colour shifts gently have their own character. Move between the busy and the calm, and find where your eye feels at ease. This, too, is a way to look at burl.

03 / LIGHT & GRAIN

What a change of light can reveal.

The same wood can appear different in colour and sheen in soft window light and beneath a desk lamp. A surface that seems quiet head-on may brighten as the angle changes. Elsewhere, an area that first appeared reflective can reveal a fine line. Moving it gently as you look offers a pleasure different from a single photograph.

With a rounded object such as a wooden barrel, turning it changes the surface in view. When a pen rests on the desk, is lifted by the fingers, or approaches the paper, the same pattern takes a different place within each distance and angle. Try alternating between the beauty of a detail and the shape of the whole.

04 / LIVING MATERIAL

Put your favourite grain into words.

“I am drawn to pale colours.” “I like broad, flowing lines.” “I could look at those fine patterns for ages.” You do not need specialist names to talk about grain. Begin by noting what catches your eye in your own words. With that small description in mind, it becomes easier to compare your preferences when you look at another piece of wood.

The way wood sits beside paper, cloth or metal is part of the pleasure of materials, too. A colour that stands out on white writing paper may look quiet beside a dark diary. Spend time looking at the pen on its own, and among the things you use each day. Both views can help you find a character that feels at home in your life.

THINGS TO KNOW

Things to know

Look at photographs from a little further back.

After exploring a close-up of the grain, zoom out and look at the whole pen. Alongside the fine pattern, notice where the light and dark areas sit and how the wood comes together with the fittings. You may discover another preference.

A small note of what you like.

When a grain catches your interest, try writing down three things you like about it: the colour, the direction of a line, the space between patterns. Unpacking the single word “beautiful” can make time spent looking at a material richer.
